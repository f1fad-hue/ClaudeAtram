Term structure · the math

How these numbers are built

Horizon vol is not read off a single quote — it is integrated out of the forward-variance curve, because variance is what adds across time, not volatility:

σ(0→T) = √( 1⁄T ∫0T σf(t)² dt )

The forward curve σf(t) is bootstrapped piecewise from the three observable points — spot VIX, the September future, the December future — then mean-reverted beyond the last liquid contract toward the long-run VIX anchor of 19.5 at speed κ = 1.5/yr. The realised-equivalent line subtracts the 3.5-point variance risk premium: options have persistently overpriced delivered volatility, and a drawdown model fed on implied vol alone would systematically overstate the damage.

1σ and 2σ are S&P-equivalent moves over the full horizon (σ√T), before any fund's volatility beta is applied.

From index vol to fund vol

Each sleeve inherits the 10-year implied level of —, scaled by its volatility beta, then combined with unhedged peso exposure. The FX leg is not just added noise — the peso tends to weaken when global risk sells off, and for a peso-based holder of USD assets that cushions the loss. Modelled at σFX 6.0%, ρ = −0.20.

σPHP = √( σA² + σFX² + 2ρσAσFX )

How this curve moves the allocation

The term structure is not just description — it is one of the four inputs the optimised portfolio is built from. The signal is the ramp: how far the horizon-blended implied vol sits above spot, using the same — weights as the regional blend. That is how much repricing the option market is still pointing at.

Each sleeve then earns or pays that ramp according to a structural sensitivity — a property of how the fund is built, not a view on the market. Only the covered-call sleeve is paid by a rising ramp.

These tilts are derived from the ramp, not hand-set, and this week shows why that matters: spot fell back toward its 2026 low while the curve held, so the ramp widened from +2.87 to — vol points and every sleeve's tilt moved with it — without anyone re-typing a number.

Turning volatility into a drawdown number

Expected maximum drawdown is modelled as (k × σ − 0.50 × μ) × √(T / 5) — volatility sets the depth, drift claws some of it back, and the horizon decides how much room the path has to find its worst peak-to-trough. The anchor is k = 1.65, calibrated so the bracket reproduces what actually happened at index level over rolling five-year windows: −20% for the S&P 500 (σ 15.5%, μ 11%) and −33% for the Nasdaq-100 (σ 24%, μ 13%), the observed medians.

A longer mandate cannot simply relabel those anchors. They describe five-year windows, and expected maximum drawdown grows with the square root of the horizon, so the whole calibrated quantity is scaled by —. That is derived from the five-year calibration, not re-fitted by eye to ten-year medians this model has not verified at a primary source — and it is why every drawdown on this page is deeper than it was when the mandate was five years.

1.65 is not the coefficient every sleeve uses. Left tails are not the same shape across these four funds, so each carries a declared adjustment — and the portfolio coefficient is the weighted average across the equity sleeves. Every drawdown printed on this page is reproducible from the k beside it; before 5 September the page showed 1.65 alone, which did not reproduce three of the four.

The money market sleeve is floored separately at a 100bp parallel rate shock on ~0.5y duration, because a cash fund cannot be made to behave like an equity fund by a formula. That floor is deliberately not horizon-scaled: a rate shock on a half-year duration book is the same size whether you hold it five years or ten.

Rationale report

Why this allocation

The recommendation is not "own more of the thing that went up." It was built from one observation — that the market was priced for calm it did not expect to keep — and that repricing has now started: spot volatility broke a 28-session range on 10 September and closed at . Exactly one of these four funds is structurally paid when that happens, which is why it carries the largest weight here. The argument does not depend on the repricing going further; it depends on the curve, which still prices by December, and on the fund being paid either way.

1 · The setup

Three central banks that matter to this portfolio are leaning the same way, and it is the wrong way for long-duration assets. The Fed held at 3.50–3.75% on a 9–3 vote in which the three dissents wanted a hike. The ECB has now finished a cycle: +25bp to 2.25% in June, its first in three years, then a second and final +25bp to on 10 September — taken explicitly because the Middle East energy shock will hold euro inflation above target for an extended period, and delivered into IMF growth of just 0.7%. The BSP hiked +25bp to 5.00% on 27 August — its third consecutive hike, +75bp cumulative since April. This is no longer a tightening bias; it is a tightening cycle, and the US is now the last piece: CME FedWatch puts the 16 September hike at , up from 70% on 10 September after a hot PPI and 58% a week ago. The 10-year closed on 10 September, a ten-year high. It is happening because Brent at — up year on year, and through intraday on 10 September, its highest since 19 May — is pushing headline inflation back up even as core behaves.

Against that, corporate earnings are still the strongest pillar in the picture. Asia ex-Japan consensus has EPS growing roughly 52% this year and 28% next, off the AI and memory cycle, on a forward multiple of 10.5×, and none of those estimates has been cut. That combination — hostile policy, hostile energy, intact earnings, undemanding valuations — is what produces a gauge on the 1–5 scale rather than a 1.9 or a 4.1.

What moved this week was not a correction. Last week the gauge fell because this model was marking down its own mistaken call that the calm was breaking. This week it fell because the calm actually broke, and because three separate things resolved against the portfolio inside four sessions: the ECB hiked, August CPI pushed the September FOMC from a coin-flip to near-certainty, and Brent went through $108 as Saudi output fell roughly 1.9 mb/d on Houthi strikes. Geopolitics & energy is cut to the floor of its scale; monetary policy and growth are both cut; volatility & risk appetite is cut as spot vol broke a 28-session range. The one driver that improved is valuation support, and it improved for the reason it always does in a week like this — the same earnings got cheaper. The gauge is a weighted read of all seven, which is why it fell by about a fifth of a point rather than falling off a cliff.

4 · The cut that pays for it

Global Technology drops from 25% to —. This is the least comfortable call on the page, because on pure strategic assumptions it has the highest gross return of the four. Three things override that.

  • It is the same bet, twice. Its correlation with Nasdaq Equity Income is 0.88. Holding both at 25%+ buys almost no diversification — you are paying two fee stacks for one exposure to US mega-cap technology.
  • It is the most rate-sensitive thing you own. Longest-duration equity, in a world where three central banks are leaning hawkish: −0.40pp.
  • It carries a −40% expected drawdown. At a 25% weight that single sleeve contributes more to portfolio drawdown than the money market and Asia sleeves combined.

Cutting it to 5% rather than zero is deliberate. The forecast deliberately sits well below the target fund's realised 15.20% five-year return, and if that AI capex cycle keeps compounding, this is the sleeve that captures it. —

5 · Cash is a position here, not a residual

The money market sleeve is cut from 20% to 15%, which looks backwards for a risk-reducing portfolio. It isn't: the drawdown control is coming from the equity mix, not from hiding in cash, so the cash can be spent on return. What justifies keeping 15% is that peso cash is genuinely paid right now in nominal terms — T-bills at 5.14% to 5.72% after a BSP that has now hiked three times to 5.00%. Be clear about what that is and isn't: against August inflation of 6.1% the real yield still runs from about −0.95pp at the 91-day bill to −0.40pp at the 364-day, so this sleeve is losing purchasing power — considerably more slowly than a month ago, when the same calculation against July's 6.2% gave roughly −1pp across the curve, but losing it. It earns its 15% on zero duration risk and on being the dry powder to rebalance into a drawdown rather than sell into one — not on being a store of real value.

Conclusion

Moving from the baseline to the optimised allocation improves both axes at once.

That is a genuine improvement on both axes at once, not a trade of one for the other — and it comes from re-weighting four funds the investor already holds, with no new products, no leverage and no market timing. The mechanism is simple enough to state in one sentence: swap concentrated long-duration tech beta for the same tech exposure with the volatility sold, and put the proceeds into the cheapest earnings growth available.

What would change this view

  • Brent back below $70 and the Fed cutting. The energy and policy drags reverse; Global Technology goes back to a full weight and the covered-call overweight loses its edge.
  • VIX futures flipping into backwardation. The premium the income sleeve harvests compresses; its — tilt goes to zero — and it is now the largest single tilt in the model, so that reversal would cost more than any other single change on this list.
  • Asia EPS revisions turning negative. The 10.5× multiple is only cheap against the growth; take the growth away and it isn't.
  • Strait of Hormuz closure. Modelled below at — — the scenario in which this portfolio's drawdown control is genuinely tested, and the one now partly under way.

Method & limits

What this model is, and is not

  • Forecasts, not facts. The 10-year CAGR figures are modelled expectations built from published capital-market assumptions and current valuations. They are not guarantees, and no fund's realised return will match them exactly.
  • One fee estimate left. The ongoing charges of the JPMorgan Asia Equity Dividend and Fidelity Global Technology target funds are taken at 1.55% and — no longer an estimate — 1.04%. Fidelity publishes the Global Technology OCF, so that one is now a verified figure. The Asia estimate is anchored on J.P. Morgan's published 1.50% management fee for the target fund and deliberately errs high; it replaced an unanchored 0.80% that had flattered the sleeve by roughly 0.75pp a year. Every other fee on this page is a published number.
  • Drawdown is expected, not worst-case. The figures describe a drawdown you should plan to experience. The stress scenarios show the tail.
  • Correlations are stable inputs to an unstable world. The 0.88 between the two US-tech sleeves rises toward 1 in a crisis, which is when diversification is worth most and delivers least.
