Term structure · the math

How these numbers are built

Horizon vol is not read off a single quote — it is integrated out of the forward-variance curve, because variance is what adds across time, not volatility:

σ(0→T) = √( 1⁄T ∫0T σf(t)² dt )

The forward curve σf(t) is bootstrapped piecewise from the three observable points — spot VIX, the September future, the December future — then mean-reverted beyond the last liquid contract toward the long-run VIX anchor of 19.5 at speed κ = 1.5/yr. The realised-equivalent line subtracts the 3.5-point variance risk premium: options have persistently overpriced delivered volatility, and a drawdown model fed on implied vol alone would systematically overstate the damage.

1σ and 2σ are S&P-equivalent moves over the full horizon (σ√T), before any fund's volatility beta is applied.

From index vol to fund vol

Each sleeve inherits the 5-year implied level of —, scaled by its volatility beta, then combined with unhedged peso exposure. The FX leg is not just added noise — the peso tends to weaken when global risk sells off, and for a peso-based holder of USD assets that cushions the loss. Modelled at σFX 6.0%, ρ = −0.20.

σPHP = √( σA² + σFX² + 2ρσAσFX )

Turning volatility into a drawdown number

Expected maximum drawdown over a rolling 5-year window is modelled as 1.65 × σ − 0.50 × μ — volatility sets the depth, drift claws some of it back. The coefficients are calibrated so the formula reproduces what actually happened: it returns −20% for the S&P 500 (σ 15.5%, μ 11%) and −33% for the Nasdaq-100 (σ 24%, μ 13%), which are the observed medians for rolling 5-year windows. The money market sleeve is floored separately at a 100bp parallel rate shock on ~0.5y duration, because a cash fund cannot be made to behave like an equity fund by a formula.

Why not simply maximise return?

Because the answer is degenerate. Maximise return alone and the optimiser puts 85% into Asia Equity — the highest macro-adjusted sleeve — for a 7.88% CAGR and a −25.5% drawdown. Rationale report

Why 15 / 45 / 35 / 5

The recommendation is not "own more of the thing that went up." It is built from one observation: the market is priced for calm it does not expect to keep, and exactly one of these four funds is structurally paid when that changes.

1 · The setup

Three central banks that matter to this portfolio are leaning the same way, and it is the wrong way for long-duration assets. The Fed held at 3.50–3.75% on a 9–3 vote in which the three dissents wanted a hike. The ECB actually delivered one — +25bp to 2.25% in June, its first in three years. The BSP hiked +25bp to 4.75%. None of this is a tightening cycle yet; all of it is a tightening bias, and it is happening because Brent at $91 — up 32% year on year after a Q1 spike to $118 — is pushing headline inflation back up even as core behaves.

Against that, corporate earnings are the strongest pillar in the picture, and it isn't close. Asia ex-Japan consensus has EPS growing roughly 52% this year and 28% next, off the AI and memory cycle, on a forward multiple of 10.5×. That combination — hostile policy, hostile energy, excellent earnings, undemanding valuations — is what produces a 5.3 gauge rather than a 3 or an 8.

2 · The signal everyone can see and few act on

Spot VIX at 14.13 is a 2026 low. The December future at 20.38 is not. Integrating the forward-variance curve gives 17.9% at three months, 19.1% at six and 19.5% at twelve — a monotonic climb of more than five volatility points from spot.

Most portfolios treat rising expected volatility purely as a reason to de-risk. That is only half the trade. A covered-call strategy sells volatility: when implied vol rises, the calls it writes fetch more premium. The Nasdaq Equity Income sleeve is therefore the single holding whose expected return improves along the exact path the futures curve is pricing — worth an estimated +0.60pp per year to its forecast, the largest single tilt in the model.

3 · The two overweights, and why they don't overlap

Asia Equity goes to 35% because it ranks first on every horizon — 6.5 / 7.0 / 7.5 / 7.5 — and because a 10.5× forward multiple against that earnings growth is the widest valuation-to-growth gap available in these four funds. It is trimmed below the unconstrained maximum for two honest reasons: Korea is already up 71% and Taiwan 49% year to date, so a good deal of the re-rating has been collected; and Asia is the region most exposed to an energy shock it imports, which costs it −0.20pp in the tilt.

Nasdaq Equity Income goes to 45% because it is the best drawdown-adjusted holding on the sheet. Option overwriting cuts its volatility to roughly two-thirds of the raw Nasdaq-100, and unhedged peso exposure trims it again, because the peso tends to weaken precisely when US equities fall. The result is 16.1% volatility against Global Technology's 24.9% — for a forecast net CAGR only 0.23pp lower.

4 · The cut that pays for it

Global Technology drops from 25% to 5%. This is the least comfortable call on the page, because on pure strategic assumptions it has the highest gross return of the four. Three things override that.

  • It is the same bet, twice. Its correlation with Nasdaq Equity Income is 0.88. Holding both at 25%+ buys almost no diversification — you are paying two fee stacks for one exposure to US mega-cap technology.
  • It is the most rate-sensitive thing you own. Longest-duration equity, in a world where three central banks are leaning hawkish: −0.40pp.
  • It carries a −41% expected drawdown. At a 25% weight that single sleeve contributes more to portfolio drawdown than the money market and Asia sleeves combined.

Cutting it to 5% rather than zero is deliberate. The forecast deliberately sits well below the target fund's realised 15.20% five-year return, and if that AI capex cycle keeps compounding, this is the sleeve that captures it. A 5% stub keeps the option alive at a cost of roughly 0.03pp of portfolio CAGR.

5 · Cash is a position here, not a residual

The money market sleeve is cut from 20% to 15%, which looks backwards for a risk-reducing portfolio. It isn't: the drawdown control is coming from the equity mix, not from hiding in cash, so the cash can be spent on return. What justifies keeping 15% is that peso cash is genuinely paid right now — T-bills at 5.14% to 5.72% against a BSP that just hiked — which is a real yield above inflation with zero duration risk, plus the dry powder to rebalance into a drawdown rather than sell into one.

Conclusion

Moving from the baseline to the optimised allocation raises forecast 5-year net CAGR from 7.17% to 7.31% while cutting expected maximum drawdown from −20.7% to −18.6%. Return per unit of drawdown improves 0.347 → 0.393, a 13% gain in the thing that actually compounds.

That is a genuine improvement on both axes at once, not a trade of one for the other — and it comes from re-weighting four funds the investor already holds, with no new products, no leverage and no market timing. The mechanism is simple enough to state in one sentence: swap concentrated long-duration tech beta for the same tech exposure with the volatility sold, and put the proceeds into the cheapest earnings growth available.

What would change this view

  • Brent back below $70 and the Fed cutting. The energy and policy drags reverse; Global Technology goes back to a full weight and the covered-call overweight loses its edge.
  • VIX futures flipping into backwardation. The premium the income sleeve harvests compresses; its +0.60pp tilt goes to zero.
  • Asia EPS revisions turning negative. The 10.5× multiple is only cheap against the growth; take the growth away and it isn't.
  • Strait of Hormuz closure. Modelled below at −34.3% — the scenario in which this portfolio's drawdown control is genuinely tested.

Method & limits

What this model is, and is not

  • Forecasts, not facts. The 5-year CAGR figures are modelled expectations built from published capital-market assumptions and current valuations. They are not guarantees, and no fund's realised return will match them exactly.
  • Two fee estimates. The ongoing charges of the JPMorgan Asia Equity Dividend and Fidelity Global Technology target funds are estimated at 0.80% and 0.95%. Every other fee on this page is a published number.
  • Drawdown is expected, not worst-case. The figures describe a drawdown you should plan to experience. The stress scenarios show the tail.
  • Correlations are stable inputs to an unstable world. The 0.88 between the two US-tech sleeves rises toward 1 in a crisis, which is when diversification is worth most and delivers least.
  • Not personal advice. This is portfolio analysis on four named funds. It does not know your tax position, your income needs, or what else you own.
